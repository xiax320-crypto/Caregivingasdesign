About This Project

This project explores the idea that design can exist in ordinary domestic actions. Our group interviewed four people about cooking, organizing, and cleaning. Although these activities may seem simple, each one involves decision-making, adjustment, problem-solving, and care for other people.

The website uses a warm home scene because most of our interviews are connected to kitchens, living rooms, and apartments. The clickable objects act like small entrances into each person’s daily design practice.

What We Learned

Design is Everyday

Design is not limited to professional studios. People design through daily routines, habits, and small decisions.

Design is Iterative

All four interviewees adjust their process over time. They try, observe, change, and improve.

Design is Emotional

Design is not only about function. It also shapes comfort, care, mood, and experience.

Reflection

At the beginning of this project, our group was mainly interested in the connection between everyday labor and design thinking. We first assumed that cooking, organizing, and cleaning could be compared to design because they involve planning and practical problem-solving. However, after completing the interviews, our understanding became more complex. The interviewees did not only talk about efficiency or function. They also talked about comfort, atmosphere, emotion, personal habits, and the needs of other people.

One of the most important things we learned is that everyday design often happens without being named as design. Most of the people we interviewed did not immediately identify themselves as designers. However, their answers clearly showed many design behaviors. They worked with constraints, considered users, balanced visual and practical elements, tested solutions, and made changes based on experience. This made us realize that design is not only a professional title. It can also be a way of thinking, noticing, and responding to life.

The interviews also changed our original research direction. At first, we focused more on function: how people arrange objects, solve problems, or complete tasks. But the interviewees often emphasized feeling and experience. Li Wei wanted people to feel cared for through food. Chen Mei wanted her home to feel relaxing instead of chaotic. Alex noticed how organization affects roommates’ moods. Maria described cleaning as something that changes how people emotionally experience a home. These answers helped us understand that design is not only about making something work. It is also about shaping how people feel.

In the website, we tried to reflect this discovery through a soft, warm, and playful visual style. The homepage is designed like a small illustrated home. Each object represents one interview and invites the viewer to enter a different part of everyday life. The cute and interactive style makes the project feel more personal and approachable, which matches the topic of ordinary people as everyday designers.

Overall, this project helped us see design in a broader way. A cook, a homemaker, a student, and a house cleaner may not describe their work with design vocabulary, but they all shape experiences for themselves and others. Their daily decisions show that design can happen quietly, repeatedly, and carefully in the background of everyday life.

Interview 1 — Cooking as Design

Interviewee: Li Wei, 48, restaurant cook

Location: In-person, home kitchen, Queens, NY

Full Interview

Mona: Hi Wei, thank you again for having us. I’ll start with my questions.

Mona: When you cook, do you ever feel like you’re “designing” something rather than just making food?

Li Wei: Well, actually, yes. Sometimes I don’t feel like I’m “cooking”; it’s more like putting things together to create a complete dish. Especially when I’m cooking at home, I have more freedom, so it feels like I’m “designing” a dish.

Mona: Do you think about how different elements come together, like a composition?

Li Wei: Yes, I do. Things like color, flavor, and texture. For example, if a dish is all soft, it feels a bit monotonous, so I’ll add something crispy to make it more interesting.

Mona: Would you say balance is important, like in design?

Li Wei: It’s very important. It can’t be too salty or too bland; sometimes even the slightest difference can throw off the whole dish. So I make adjustments as I go to get it just right.

Mona: Do you ever adjust things while cooking, like designers refine their work?

Li Wei: I do that all the time. I rarely follow a fixed set of steps; I taste as I go and make changes. For example, if I feel it’s not flavorful enough, I’ll add a little more seasoning and keep tweaking until I’m satisfied.

Yi: Do you think cooking is similar to solving a problem?

Li Wei: Yes, it’s very similar. Sometimes, depending on what ingredients you have at home, you have to figure out how to combine them—you can’t always use your ideal ingredients.

Yi: So it’s like working with constraints?

Li Wei: Yes, exactly—working within limited conditions. For example, if you’re missing an ingredient, you have to find a way to substitute it. That process is actually quite similar to problem-solving.

Yi: Do you plan first, or just start and adjust?

Li Wei: Usually I have a general idea—like what I want to make today—but as for the specifics, I often figure it out as I go and make adjustments based on the situation.

Yi: Would you say that’s similar to a design process?

Li Wei: I think it’s quite similar. It’s not something that’s completely set in stone from the start; rather, it’s something that’s gradually refined through adjustments.

Xinyi: Do you think about who you’re cooking for, the way designers think about users?

Li Wei: Yes, I do. That’s very important. When cooking for different people, the approach is definitely different.

Xinyi: How does that affect your decisions?

Li Wei: For example, if I’m cooking for the elderly, I’ll make it lighter and softer; for children, I might make it a bit more fun and colorful. I adjust it based on their preferences.

Xinyi: Do you think emotion plays a role—like how people feel when they eat?

Li Wei: Yes, it does. I want them to feel relaxed and happy while eating, not just full.

Xinyi: So it’s not just functional, but also emotional?

Li Wei: Right. It’s not just about filling their stomachs; it’s about making them feel comfortable, and sometimes even a little bit cared for.

Annie: Do you think experience matters more than following recipes?

Li Wei: I think so. At first, you might need to follow a recipe, but after cooking for a while, you develop your own sense of what works and know what feels right.

Annie: Do you still use recipes?

Li Wei: I’ll glance at them occasionally, but I don’t follow them exactly. I mostly use them as a reference and then adapt them myself.

Annie: Would you say cooking is like an iterative process?

Li Wei: Yes, every time I cook, it turns out a little differently, and it gradually gets a little better.

Annie: If you had to describe cooking as design in one sentence, what would you say?

Li Wei: I think cooking is really about using food to create an experience for others—to leave them with a certain feeling after eating, rather than just having had a meal.

Interview 2 — Organizing Space as Design

Interviewee: Chen Mei, 45, homemaker

Location: In-person, living room, family apartment

Full Interview

Mona: When you organize your home, do you feel like you’re designing the space?

Chen Mei: Well, actually, I do get that feeling. Because every time I tidy up, I think about where things should go and how to arrange them for maximum convenience—it’s not just about tidying up.

Mona: Do you think about how things look and how they work together?

Chen Mei: Yes, I do. For example, if things are placed too haphazardly, it looks messy and uncomfortable, but if they’re just pretty but not practical, that won’t work either. So I usually have to consider both aspects.

Mona: Do you try to create a kind of balance in your space?

Chen Mei: Yes, I don’t like it when things feel too cramped, nor do I like it when there’s too much empty space. Too many things create clutter, while too much empty space feels lifeless, so it has to be just right.

Mona: Do you adjust things over time?

Chen Mei: I change things around often. Sometimes I think a certain arrangement works well at first, but after using it for a while, I realize it’s not very convenient, so I’ll rearrange it.

Yi: Do you think organizing is like solving a problem?

Chen Mei: I think so, especially when you don’t have a lot of space at home. Every time I organize, it’s like figuring out how to arrange things.

Yi: Like working with constraints?

Chen Mei: Yes, it’s very obvious in small apartments. You can’t just put things anywhere; you have to figure out how to use the space, otherwise it gets messy very quickly.

Yi: Do you plan ahead or just experiment?

Chen Mei: I might have a rough idea at the start, but most of the time I just try things out as I go. For example, if something isn’t convenient where I put it, I’ll move it somewhere else and try again.

Yi: So it’s kind of a process?

Chen Mei: Yes, it’s something I adjust gradually—it doesn’t get sorted out in one go.

Xinyi: Do you think about your family when organizing?

Chen Mei: Yes, that’s very important. I don’t just organize things based on my own habits.

Xinyi: How does that affect your decisions?

Chen Mei: For example, I put things my children use often where they can easily reach them, and I also make things the elderly use more accessible, so everything works more smoothly for everyone.

Xinyi: Do you think about how the space feels emotionally?

Chen Mei: Yes, I do. I want our home to feel relaxing, not chaotic or oppressive the moment you walk in.

Xinyi: So it’s also about the experience?

Chen Mei: Yes, actually. It’s not just about “storing things”; it’s also about making it comfortable to live in.

Annie: Have your habits changed over time?

Chen Mei: They’ve changed a lot. I used to care more about how things looked, but now I care more about how practical they are.

Annie: What changed?

Chen Mei: Maybe after living here for a while, I realized that some ways of organizing things that look neat are actually quite inconvenient to use, so I’ve gradually adjusted them.

Annie: Do you learn from your mistakes?

Chen Mei: Yes, I do. For example, if I put something in the wrong place at first and it’s really inconvenient to use, I won’t put it there next time.

Annie: Would you say organizing is a form of design?

Chen Mei: I think so, because you’re constantly adjusting the space based on your life to make it better suited for people.

Interview 3 — Learning Through Practice as Design

Interviewee: Alex Rivera, 22, college student

Location: Online interview via Zoom

Full Interview

Mona: Living as a student usually means dealing with limited space and resources. Do you ever feel like you’re “designing” your daily environment?

Alex: Yeah, honestly, especially because my apartment is really small. I’m constantly figuring out how to make things fit and work better. Sometimes it feels less like cleaning and more like trying to redesign the space so it’s easier to live in.

Mona: What kinds of things do you pay attention to when organizing your space?

Alex: Mostly convenience. Like, I think about what I use every day and where it makes the most sense to put it. But I also care about how the space feels. If everything is too crowded, I get stressed really quickly.

Mona: Do you think your routines changed after living independently?

Alex: Definitely. Before moving out, I never really thought about how much effort goes into maintaining a space. Now I notice that even small things—like where I leave my bag or dishes—can affect the whole environment.

Mona: Do you adjust your systems over time?

Alex: All the time. I think I found a good setup, but after a few weeks I realize something isn’t practical, so I change it again. It’s kind of trial and error.

Yi: Do you think working with a small apartment feels similar to designing under constraints?

Alex: Yeah, for sure. Since I don’t have much room, every area has to serve multiple purposes. My desk is for studying, eating, and sometimes even storage. You have to get creative.

Yi: Do you usually plan your organization carefully or improvise more?

Alex: I usually start with an idea, but honestly most of it happens while I’m doing it. I move things around a lot until it finally feels right.

Yi: Do you think mistakes help you improve your space?

Alex: Definitely. Sometimes I organize things in a way that looks nice at first, but later I realize it’s annoying to use every day. Then I learn from that and change it.

Yi: So would you say the process is iterative?

Alex: Yeah, exactly. It never really feels finished. I’m always adjusting something depending on what’s working or not working.

Xinyi: Since you live with roommates, do you think about other people when organizing shared spaces?

Alex: Yes, a lot actually. You can’t only think about your own habits because everyone uses the same kitchen and living room.

Xinyi: How does that affect your decisions?

Alex: I try to keep shared things in places everyone can reach easily, and I also try not to take up too much space. Otherwise it can create tension really quickly.

Xinyi: Do you think organization affects the atmosphere between people?

Alex: Yeah, definitely. When the apartment is messy, everyone gets irritated more easily. But when the space feels clean and organized, it actually changes people’s moods.

Xinyi: So it’s also about creating a better shared experience?

Alex: Yes, exactly. A space isn’t just functional—it also affects how people feel living together.

Annie: Have your habits changed since you first moved out?

Alex: A lot. At first I cared more about making things look aesthetic, but now I focus more on practicality and comfort.

Annie: What made you change your perspective?

Alex: I realized that if something looks good but is annoying to maintain, it doesn’t really work long term. Living somewhere every day changes how you think about space.

Annie: Do you think you’ve learned through experience?

Alex: Yeah, completely. Nobody really teaches you these things—you learn by making mistakes and figuring out what works for your own lifestyle.

Annie: Would you describe organizing daily life as a form of design?

Alex: I think so, because you’re constantly shaping your environment around your needs and trying to improve how people live in that space.

Interview 4 — Cleaning and Atmosphere as Design

Interviewee: Maria Lopez, 52, house cleaner

Location: In-person, client’s apartment

Full Interview

Mona: When you clean a space, do you ever feel like you’re transforming or redesigning it?

Maria: Yes, definitely. Cleaning isn’t only about removing dirt. When a space is clean and organized, it completely changes how it feels and how people move through it.

Mona: What do you usually pay attention to while cleaning?

Maria: I pay attention to both appearance and comfort. For example, I think about whether the room feels open, calm, and easy to use after I finish.

Mona: Do you think small details matter?

Maria: Very much. Even small things like how pillows are placed or how objects are arranged can change the atmosphere of a room.

Mona: Do you make adjustments while working?

Maria: Always. Every home is different, and sometimes I realize a different arrangement works better once I’m actually in the space.

Yi: Do you think cleaning involves problem-solving?

Maria: Yes, all the time. Sometimes spaces are very crowded or difficult to organize, so I have to figure out practical solutions.

Yi: Do different homes create different challenges?

Maria: Definitely. Some homes are small, some have children, some have pets. Every space has different needs, so I can’t clean every home the same way.

Yi: Do you usually follow a system or improvise?

Maria: I have a general routine, but I also adapt depending on the situation and what the client needs most.

Yi: So cleaning is more like an ongoing process?

Maria: Yes, because spaces are always changing as people live in them.

Xinyi: Do you think about the people who live in the space while cleaning?

Maria: Yes, that’s one of the most important parts. Different people have different habits and lifestyles.

Xinyi: How does that affect your decisions?

Maria: For example, if a family has children, I try to make things safer and easier to access. If someone is older, I think more about comfort and convenience.

Xinyi: Do you think cleaning changes people emotionally?

Maria: Yes. When people come home to a clean space, they usually feel more relaxed and peaceful. The environment affects emotions a lot more than people realize.

Xinyi: So cleaning is also about creating an emotional experience?

Maria: Yes, exactly. It’s not just physical work—it also changes how people experience their home.

Annie: Have your cleaning habits changed over the years?

Maria: Yes, very much. I notice details now that I didn’t notice before, and I think more carefully about how people actually use their spaces.

Annie: What helped you improve over time?

Maria: Experience mostly. After working in many homes, I’ve learned what kinds of arrangements make spaces feel more comfortable and functional.

Annie: Do you think experience teaches you more than instructions?

Maria: Yes, because every home teaches you something different. You learn by observing people and understanding how they live.

Annie: Would you say cleaning can be considered a form of design?

Maria: I think so, because you’re shaping the environment to better support the people living in it.
